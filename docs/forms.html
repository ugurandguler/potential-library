Four forms compared

What happens to a φ2 + φ3 potential for bcc transition metals when the three-body term is replaced by a second-moment embedding, and what happens when Legendre partial densities are added to it. Five elements, ten binaries, and a five-species alloy.

These are not potentials offered for use. They are a controlled comparison of functional forms at matched parameter counts: the same data, the same objective, the same hold-out. They cover five of the library's thirty-eight elements, they are not implemented in pair_ugur, and no parameter file is shipped for them. Nothing on this page changes the library. The surface energies are still wrong by tens of per cent, the facet ordering is still wrong for tungsten and molybdenum, and the mixing energies below show what they cannot do at all.

The question, and the forms

The published form for these metals is a pair term plus a three-body term. Two of its answers are wrong in opposite directions: the vacancy comes out too expensive and the migration barrier far too cheap, and both are properties of what the energy does when an atom’s coordination changes rather than when a single bond stretches. That is the job the three-body term is doing. So the experiment is to take it out, put a second-moment embedding in its place, change nothing else, and see which half of the form was buying what.

All four forms are built on one shape function, so the pair term is common to all of them and the comparison is not confounded by it:

f(x) = (r0/x)γ · [ emα(r0−x) − m eα(r0−x) ] / (m − 1)

Every term is cut off by the same quintic switch, which is smooth to second order at both ends and starts at 85 per cent of the cutoff:

S(r) = 1 − t3(10 − 15t + 6t2),   t = (r − 0.85 rc) / (0.15 rc), clipped to [0, 1]

MAU — the published form, 9 parameters

E = ½∑ij D f(rij) S(rij)  +  ∑i ∑j<k C D f(rij+rik; α3) S(rij) S(rik)

The three-body term takes the sum of the two legs as its argument, and uses the same shape function with its own range α3. Free: D, α, r0, m, γ, C, α3 and the two cutoffs.

UG — the published angular factor, 11 parameters

… × h(cosθjik) = 1 + λ2P2(cosθ) + λ4P4(cosθ)

The same three-body term multiplied by a Legendre factor in the angle at the central atom. Adds λ2 and λ4.

FS — the second-moment embedding, 9 parameters

E = ½∑ij D f(rij) S(rij)  +  ∑i F(ρ̄i),   F(ρ̄) = −A√ρ̄

ρ̄i = ρ0,i = ∑j fij,   fij = e−β(rij−r0) S(rij; rcd)

The three-body term is gone. In its place each atom sums a density over its neighbours and the energy is the square root of it — the second moment of a tight-binding band. The pair term is unchanged, and the parameter count is the same nine: A and β and a density cutoff replace C, α3 and rc3.

UG-2 — the embedding with Legendre partial densities, 11 parameters

ρ̄i2 = ρ0,i2 + t2Q2,i + t4Q4,i,   Ql,i = ∑jk fij fik Pl(cosθjik)

The density gains angular content. Ql is never negative: by the spherical-harmonic addition theorem the double sum is a sum of squares, which is what keeps the square root real without a floor being imposed by hand. Setting t2 = t4 = 0 gives back FS exactly, so the two differ by two parameters and nothing else — the same relation UG has to MAU.

Note what is not the same between the two angular constructions. UG multiplies an energy by an angular factor, so a single triplet can lower the energy or raise it. UG-2 puts the angle inside the density, under a square root that is already concave in coordination. That is why the two behave differently even though both are “Legendre corrections”.

What the comparison established

The limit was the parameter count, not the form. A free spline φ2 + φ3 potential fitted by linear regression sits three to three and a half times below the best of these forms on held-out forces, and further below the rest, consistently across five elements and two groups of the periodic table. So no claim of the kind “a two- and three-body form cannot do this” is supported. What survives is a statement about compactness.

The advantage is about the anchors, not about forces. Refit the same four forms with the experimental anchors switched off and they come within ten per cent of one another, with UG-2 third rather than first. The embedding with Legendre densities satisfies the anchors nearly for free on both elements tested (a fifth on niobium, 3 % on molybdenum); the angle-free and angular three-body forms pay two fifths to three quarters, and the plain embedding pays all of it on niobium and almost nothing on molybdenum. Carrying the anchors is what an empirical potential is for, so this is the narrower and more useful claim.

In alloys the added densities earn their place against the plain embedding. With the cross term fitted to binary forces only, UG-2 beats it in seven of ten binaries, ties in two and loses in one. Against all four forms it is the best in six; in the other four (Mo-Nb, Mo-Ta, Mo-V, Nb-V) one of the two three-body forms is ahead of it. The cross terms then transfer, unchanged and with no free parameter, to three, four and five species. Mechanical stability agrees: with a fitted cross term UG-2 has no violation deeper than relaxation noise anywhere.

Established potentials do better on the same data. Run on exactly the same hold-out configurations, published potentials that never saw these data beat the best of the four forms on every element. The second-neighbour MEAM of Lee and co-workers, fitted to measured elastic constants, vacancy and surface energies with thirteen or fourteen parameters per element, beats UG-2 on 5 of 5 and is the best published potential on 3; the gap is mostly in the free surfaces. For alloys the picture is mixed: on Mo-Ta, Mo-W, Ta-W Zhou's 2004 database is better, although its cross terms are not fitted at all but built by a mixing rule, while on Nb-Ta, Nb-V, Ta-V, V-W a cross term fitted here beats every published potential that covers the pair. So the forms on this page are a study of what an embedding changes, not a replacement for an established potential.

Chemical energetics do not transfer. Where the density-functional energy differences between arrangements of the same composition are small, every form errs by four to six times the signal, and the two embedding forms are anti-correlated with the reference. The mixing energy of the five-species alloy shows the same thing plainly: three of the four forms predict that the alloy cannot form at all. No ordering, segregation or phase-stability claim follows from any of this work.

How these numbers were produced

The fits

All four forms were fitted to the same data with the same objective, the same hold-out and matched parameter counts — nine against nine, eleven against eleven — so that the only thing that changes between columns is the form. The objective is a least-squares sum of three blocks: forces at weight 1, energies per atom at weight 20 with one free constant per atom solved exactly rather than fitted, and the experimental anchors — cohesive energy, lattice constant and the elastic constants — at a weight written Wexp.

The number in parentheses beside each element is its anchor weight. It is not a free choice. The fitting script’s default is 40, at which every form sacrifices the anchors and misses C11 and C44 by tens of GPa — and at which the ranking of the forms is not the same as at the knee. The knee measured on the tungsten set is 240, and carrying it to each training set by the block-scale rule Wexp = 240 √(nFσF2 / 2.245×104) gives W 240, Nb 272, Mo 380, Ta 385, V 248. Every single-element number on this page is at its own knee, except where the table says the anchors were switched off.

The hold-out

Tungsten was fitted to 280 configurations of the W-14 set and scored on 280 more. Molybdenum, niobium, tantalum and vanadium were fitted to 464 configurations each, subsampled by configuration type with a fixed quota and a fixed seed; for those four the hold-out is not a random split but three categories the fit never sees — the (112) surface, the C15 structure and the di-vacancy. A random split would measure interpolation; withholding whole categories measures what the fit does with a structure it has not met.

The alloys

For every binary the five single-element fits stay frozen at their knee weights and only the five cross-pair parameters are free — the pair depth, its range, its minimum, and the two exponents. The binaries use all 39 configurations of each pair that are neither liquid nor dimer, five-fold cross-validated so that every configuration is held out exactly once. The ternary fit frees fifteen to seventeen parameters across three unlike pairs and is scored on the dataset authors’ own test file. The transfer table fits nothing at all: the cross terms are used exactly as the binaries left them, on configurations with three, four and five species from a file no fit here has touched.

The screens

Bain is the volume-conserving tetragonal path through a 54-atom random 50:50 solution with the positions relaxed at every c/a and two chemical arrangements per pair; a pair counts as cubic when c/a = 1 is a local minimum and the face-centred end of the path is uphill, and the reference volume is the alloy’s own energy minimum rather than an experimental lattice table. That path probes only the tetragonal shear, so a form can pass it with a negative C44; the Born row therefore computes relaxed C11−C12, C44 and B from three strain modes and requires all three to be positive.

Point defects are computed in a 128-atom random equiatomic cell at its own relaxed volume, one removed site per species, three arrangements. The migration energy is the maximum of a constrained path — the hopping atom pinned at nine fractions of the way to the vacancy while everything else relaxes — because in a random alloy the midpoint of a jump is not a saddle: there is no symmetry to make it one, and pinning an atom there lets the rest rearrange into a state degenerate with the vacancy. The number is therefore a lower bound on the true barrier.

Intervals, and what “n.s.” means

Every percentage carries a 95 per cent interval from a paired bootstrap over hold-out configurations — 4000 resamples, the two forms scored on the same resampled set each time, so the comparison is paired rather than two independent error bars. n.s. marks an interval that contains zero. Pooled root-mean-square weights the hardest configurations most, which is why the per-pair rows matter: a group average can be carried almost entirely by one difficult pair.

Verification

Analytic forces were checked against finite differences for every form and agree to better than 10−8 eV/Å throughout, and to 10−9 for the free-spline one; the multi-species evaluator reproduces the single-species one to 10−14; and the spline fitting machinery recovers a known potential to 0.0002 eV/Å on a synthetic hold-out. Each table below states what was computed for it and on how much data.

What is not claimed

None of these fits is a usable potential. The alloy results speak about forces and mechanical stability and not about formation energies or phase diagrams. The liquid results show the gain disappearing, so nothing here carries into melting or transport. And no claim of the form “alloys can be predicted from the pure elements” is supported: the cross term was fitted, to alloy data, one binary at a time. What the transfer test shows is narrower and still worth having — that a cross term fitted on binaries carries to three, four and five species without refitting.
